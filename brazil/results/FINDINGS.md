# Brazil: the Chamber without its floor and ceiling

Nine Chamber elections, 1990-2022. Generated by `brazil/tools/chamber.py`;
raw numbers in `chamber_tse.json` and `chamber_passport.json`.

## The question

Article 45 of the 1988 Constitution makes Chamber seats proportional to state
population and then contradicts itself: **no state may have fewer than 8 or
more than 70**. This holds everything else fixed -- the same votes, the same
chamber size, the same within-state allocation rule -- and changes only that:
a floor of 1, no ceiling.

Seats are apportioned on the most recent census before each election, which is
how apportionment normally works and keeps an interpolated population series
out of the comparison. The Chamber had **503 seats in 1990** and 513 from 1994,
after LC 78/1993.

The 1990 and 1994 seat tables come from pt.wikipedia and are verified twice
over: every row sums to its published state total, and the states sum to 503
and 513. They differ in **exactly one state**. Sao Paulo had **60** seats in
1990 and 70 in 1994; every other state is unchanged. So the entire ten-seat
expansion of LC 78/1993 went to Sao Paulo, lifting it to the constitutional
ceiling. The 1994 table also reproduces the LC 78/1993 numbers used throughout
this analysis, which is independent confirmation of them.

## 1. There are two distortions, not one

A scenario was added that was not in the original design and turned out to
matter: **recompute the constitutional rule itself** -- floor 8, ceiling 70 --
on current population. It does *not* reproduce the real seat table. Eleven to
fourteen states differ in every census:

| Census | States differing from the real table | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1991 (503 seats, 1990) | 12 | GO has 17, rule gives 14; ES has 10, rule gives 9; PB has 12, rule gives 11 |
| 1991 | 11 | BA has 39, rule gives 42; ES has 10, rule gives 9; GO has 17, rule gives 14 |
| 2000 | 14 | AM has 8, rule gives 9; BA has 39, rule gives 40; CE has 22, rule gives 23 |
| 2010 | 13 | AM has 8, rule gives 10; CE has 22, rule gives 23; GO has 17, rule gives 16 |
| 2022 | 14 | AL has 9, rule gives 8; AM has 8, rule gives 10; BA has 39, rule gives 37 |

So Brazil's apportionment is distorted twice over:

1. **The freeze.** The table has not moved since LC 78/1993. Para should have
   21 seats on the 2010 census and has 17; Amazonas should have 10 and has 8.
   This is not the Constitution's rule -- it is the rule's *1993 output*,
   left in place for thirty years.
2. **The floor and ceiling**, which is the rule itself.

The three scenarios separate them: `actual` -> `recomputed_with_limits`
isolates the freeze; `recomputed_with_limits` -> `no_limits` isolates the
constitutional limits.

## 2. What the limits do to representation

Worst-to-best ratio of people per seat:

| Census | Real (frozen) table | Rule recomputed | Floor 1, no ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1991, 503 seats (1990) | **19.36x** | 16.59x | 1.92x |
| 1991 | 16.59x | 16.59x | 1.47x |
| 2000 | 13.05x | 13.05x | 1.71x |
| 2010 | 10.47x | 10.47x | 1.35x |
| 2022 | 7.97x | 7.97x | 1.30x |

For 1994 onwards the first two columns are identical, because the ratio is set
entirely by the two binding constraints -- Roraima at the floor, Sao Paulo at
the ceiling -- and both are pinned in both scenarios. **The freeze
redistributes unfairness among the middle states; the limits create it at the
extremes.**

1990 is the exception, and the worst year in the series at **19.36x**: Sao
Paulo was held to 60 rather than the constitutional 70, so the ceiling bit
harder still. A Roraima vote was worth nineteen Sao Paulo votes. Removing the
limits brings that to 1.92x, with Sao Paulo going from 60 seats to **108** and
Roraima, Acre and Amapa from 8 to 1.

### The floor of 1 never binds

Worth stating because it is easy to assume otherwise from the seat counts: a
state dropping to 1 seat does not show that the floor caught it. Running the
apportionment with **no floor at all** settles it, and in all nine elections
**no state ever reaches zero** -- Roraima earns its single seat on population
in every year, and two in 2022:

| Year | Seats | Smallest four, no floor and no ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 503 | RR 1, AP 1, AC 1, TO 3 |
| 1994-1998 | 513 | RR 1, AP 1, AC 2, TO 3 |
| 2002-2006 | 513 | RR 1, AP 1, AC 2, TO 4 |
| 2010-2018 | 513 | RR 1, AP 2, AC 2, TO 4 |
| 2022 | 513 | RR 2, AP 2, AC 2, TO 4 |

So the "floor 1" in the counterfactual is decorative: it is the *ceiling of 70*
and the *floor of 8* that do all the work, and the scenario is equivalent to
removing both limits outright. A floor of 1 would only start to bite if the
chamber were much smaller or a unit much tinier -- neither is the case here.

On the coalition-level series, removing the floor and ceiling cuts
disproportionality by only **13%**. Pooling the country into a single
district cuts it by **84%**.

This is the finding worth arguing about, because it separates two things that
are routinely conflated. Brazil's apportionment is an enormous violation of
one-person-one-vote -- an 8-to-16x weight ratio between citizens, depending on
the census -- and it is nonetheless only a **minor** source of partisan
disproportionality. The over-represented states are not a single partisan bloc,
so their gains and losses largely cancel at party level.

What does drive disproportionality is **district magnitude**. Fourteen of the
27 units elect 10 members or fewer, and an electoral quotient in an 8-seat
district is 12.5% of the vote -- a threshold far above anything Brazil imposes
explicitly. Small parties are excluded by arithmetic, not by rule.

**The floor and ceiling are an equality-of-voters problem, not a
proportionality problem.** They should be argued about on that ground, and
anyone claiming the cap on Sao Paulo distorts the party system has the numbers
against them.

Two patterns hold across both series and every election:

- **The losers are the big federative parties** -- PFL/DEM/UNIAO, PMDB/MDB, PP
  -- strongest in the over-represented North and Northeast.
- **The winners are the more urban, Southeast-based parties** -- PT, PSDB,
  PSOL, PV. Real, consistent, and small: 2-8 seats.

The national-district column is a different and larger story: it **shrinks the
largest parties substantially** and lets several more in. Low-magnitude
districts over-reward whoever is locally largest, and that outweighs the
apportionment.

## Sources, and why there are two series

| | |
|---|---|
| TSE `votacao_partido_munzona` | votes by party, municipality and zone; **coalition-level**; 1998-2022 |
| Election Passport (David Lublin) `BR.xlsx` | votes by party per state; **party-level**; 1990-2014 |
| IBGE | censuses 1991, 2000, 2010, 2022, via the SIDRA API |

Seats were won by *coalitions* until 2018, so the TSE series applies Brazil's
actual rule. Election Passport gives party totals only, so its series applies
a party-level rule that Brazil never used -- but it is the **only source for
1990**, and it is an independent compilation, so where the two overlap it
cross-checks the TSE parsing rather than merely extending it.

**1994 is in the Passport series and not the TSE one.** TSE's 1994 file records
*zero legenda votes in every state* -- party-list votes are simply absent, and
Sao Paulo's total is 8.66M against a true 10.65M. The file is unusable for
seat allocation and is excluded rather than silently used.

## Validation

**Cross-source agreement** on total valid votes, after fixing two parsing bugs
described below:

| Year | TSE | Election Passport | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1998 | 66,599,875 | 66,600,077 | -0.000% |
| 2002 | 87,474,543 | 87,549,923 | -0.086% |
| 2006 | 93,617,425 | 93,184,687 | +0.464% |
| 2010 | 98,389,861 | 98,313,670 | +0.077% |
| 2014 | 97,355,354 | 97,295,944 | +0.061% |

Two independent compilations agreeing to within half a percent -- and to
**0.000%** in 1998 -- is the strongest check available, since neither derives
from the other.

**Two bugs this comparison caught**, both silent:

1. Each TSE archive contains per-state files *and* `_BRASIL.csv` / `_BR.csv`
   aggregates that repeat every state's rows with the real `SG_UF` on them.
   Reading the whole archive counted the country twice. Because the
   duplication was proportional it did not move the seat allocations, but the
   vote totals were 2x wrong and nothing in the output said so.
2. The 1998 archive ships **no DF file at all**; the Federal District exists
   only inside `_BRASIL.csv`. Its million votes were missing until the reader
   was taught to fall back to the aggregate for states it omits.

**2022 party seats** under the actual apportionment, against the real result:
PDT (17), PSB (14), PSOL (12) and REPUBLICANOS (41) exact; PT gives 75 against
68 and PSDB 14 against 18. Those two are precisely the *federacoes*, which fill
seats by candidate ranking regardless of party -- the documented approximation,
not a parsing error.

**1990 party seats** under the real 503-seat table, against the real Chamber:
31 of 503 seats misallocated, **6.2%**. The direction is
diagnostic -- PFL +11 and PMDB +6, with every small party short -- which is
exactly what dropping coalitions does: small parties fall under the electoral
quotient alone and their votes flow to the largest party by largest averages.
Treat the 1990 party columns as indicative to a few seats; the seats-per-state
numbers do not depend on any of it.

## What is still missing

- **One seat in the 1994 seat table, in the source itself.** The tables are now
  parsed from wikitext rather than read off a screenshot, and 1990 reconciles
  completely -- every row against its published Soma, every party column
  against its published Total, the states against 503. 1994 reconciles on rows
  and on the chamber total but *not* on two columns: its own cells give PMDB
  106 against a published 107 and PSDB 64 against 63. One seat is attributed to
  the wrong party somewhere in the source. The apportionment is unaffected and
  the party cells feed nothing, so it is recorded rather than guessed at.
- **Coalition membership before 2018 in the Passport series**, and candidate
  votes generally. `votacao_candidato_munzona` would fix both the 1990
  coalition problem and the federation approximation; it is 106 MB for 1998
  alone.
- **2018 and 2022 in the Passport series** -- it stops at 2014.
