It is more compact, and where it is not is informative

Compactness is what splitline claims, so it should be measured rather than assumed. The measure here is the average distance from a resident to the centre of their own district, population-weighted — which is also Brian Olson's objective. It is used because it needs no district outlines: the enacted map arrives as a list of census blocks with no boundaries attached, so perimeter-based scores cannot be computed for the very map the comparison needs.

The states where splitline wins biggest are the maps of the decade that were litigated, redrawn by courts, or both. The measure was chosen for being computable on both maps, not for detecting gerrymanders, and it finds them anyway.

Washington is the exception and it is not noise. Its enacted map was drawn by a bipartisan commission, so there was no gerrymander to undo; and its population sits in one dense strip along Puget Sound with a mountain range and an empty east behind it. A rule that cuts on the shortest straight line runs those cuts through the strip and pairs Seattle neighbourhoods with hundreds of kilometres of the Cascades, which a commission drawing to communities of interest will not do. Splitline beats a map drawn to win seats and loses to a map drawn to respect geography.
