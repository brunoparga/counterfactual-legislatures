# What the counterfactual maps actually produce

Generated by `usa/tools/score_matrix.py` and `usa/tools/demographics.py`.
Raw per-district numbers are in the JSON files beside this one; `index.json`
lists every scoring with its national summary.

## What is being measured, and what is not

**The vote is presidential, not congressional.** Block-level House returns
barely exist — 26 states in 2024, 21 in 2022, 3 in 2016 — so there is no
national House map at this resolution and there is not going to be.
Presidential vote is the standard substitute in redistricting work and it has
a genuine advantage here: it is the same contest in every district, so it does
not confound districting with incumbency, candidate quality, or uncontested
seats. It measures **the partisan balance of the ground a seat is drawn on**,
not who would have won the seat.

The distinction is not pedantic. In 2012 Obama carried 209 districts under the
enacted map, but Democrats won only 201 seats. An eight-seat gap between "the
district leans D" and "the D candidate wins" is normal, and every number below
is of the first kind.

**"Bias" here means seat share minus vote share**, in percentage points,
positive for Democrats. It is a descriptive quantity, not the partisan-symmetry
measure of the political-science literature, which requires a swing model.

## 1. Splitline removes about two thirds of the pro-Republican bias

2010-census districts, scored four ways. `enacted_cd113` is the plan actually
in force; `splitline_2010` is ours. Same votes, same states, same blocks —
only the lines differ.

| Contest | D vote % | Enacted D | bias | Splitline D | bias | swing | control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 pres | 51.86 | 209 | −3.81 | **231** | +1.24 | +22 | R → **D flips** |
| 2016 pres | 51.01 | 202 | −4.57 | 215 | −1.58 | +13 | R → R |
| 2020 pres | 52.18 | 221 | −1.37 | 229 | +0.47 | +8 | D → D |
| 2016-20 composite | 51.68 | 215 | −2.26 | **221** | −0.88 | +6 | R → **D flips** |

Mean absolute bias falls from **3.00 to 1.04 points — a 65% reduction**. The
direction is consistent: splitline moves seats toward Democrats in all four
scorings, by between 6 and 22 seats.

**Two of the four flip chamber control**, both of them cases where Democrats
carried the national two-party presidential vote but the enacted map returned
a Republican-leaning chamber.

### The interesting failure: 2016 does not flip

Democrats carried the 2016 two-party presidential vote 51.0–49.0 and would
still have held only **215 of 435** districts under splitline — three short of
a majority. Splitline moves 13 seats and that is not enough.

This is the result worth dwelling on, because it separates two explanations
that are usually conflated. The Democratic seat deficit is **not** purely a
gerrymandering artifact. A large part of it is **political geography**:
Democratic voters are concentrated in dense urban areas and win those
districts with enormous margins, wasting votes, while Republican margins are
spread more efficiently across suburban and rural districts. A compactness-
maximising, party-blind rule cannot fix that, because the concentration is
real and compact districts respect it.

So the honest summary is: **redistricting reform recovers roughly two thirds
of the measured bias, and the remaining third is geography, not cartography.**

## 2. The 2000-census map, and a caveat about it

| Plan | Contest | D vote % | D districts | bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| splitline_2000 | 2008 pres | 53.56 | 257 | +5.52 |

No enacted comparator: we hold the block equivalency file for the 113th
Congress only, so there is nothing to difference this against. A **+5.5 bias
in the opposite direction** in a Democratic wave year suggests splitline is
not neutral so much as *responsive* — it converts vote swings into seat swings
more aggressively than the enacted maps do, which is a distinct property from
being unbiased and should not be reported as fairness. Confirming it needs the
BEFs for the 108th–112th Congresses.

**The 2024 row is not a national result and must not be quoted as one.** DRA
publishes no 2024 block file for AR, CT, ID, ME, MI, NJ, OK, OR or PA — nine
states, 66 districts, including Michigan and Pennsylvania. This is an upstream
gap, not a stale download: those states have no newer version published (the
DRA server rejects `HEAD`, so this had to be checked with ranged `GET`s).

## 4. Splitline is race-blind and it is not race-neutral

Voting-age population by race and Hispanic origin, PL 94-171 table P4, 2010
census, all 435 districts. Counts are districts in which the group exceeds the
stated share of VAP.

| Group | National VAP % | \>50% enacted | \>50% splitline | change | 40–50% | 30–40% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hispanic | 14.23 | 30 | 22 | **−8** | 9 → 14 | 19 → 23 |
| non-Hisp. White | 67.04 | 338 | 346 | **+8** | 23 → 33 | 32 → 30 |
| non-Hisp. Black | 11.57 | 22 | **5** | **−17** | 9 → 12 | 11 → 26 |
| non-Hisp. Asian | 4.81 | 1 | 0 | −1 | 1 → 2 | 4 → 4 |

**Black-majority districts fall from 22 to 5, a 77% reduction.** Districts
where Black voters are 30–40% of VAP more than double, 11 to 26. The
population is not disappearing; it is being spread across more districts in
concentrations too small to elect a candidate of choice.

This is the mechanism the Voting Rights Act §2 exists to prevent, and it is
the predictable consequence of a rule that cuts on the shortest available
straight line. Majority-minority districts are frequently non-compact by
necessity — they follow a river, a rail corridor, a chain of neighbourhoods —
and an algorithm blind to race by construction cannot see them.

**These two findings are the same phenomenon seen from two sides.** Majority-
minority districts pack the most reliably Democratic voters in the country
into a small number of seats won with 80% margins. Dissolving them is
*simultaneously* why splitline gains Democrats seats and why it destroys Black
descriptive representation. A reform that improves partisan proportionality by
unpacking the Democratic Party's most loyal constituency is not obviously a
reform, and the trade-off should be stated in exactly those terms rather than
resolved by picking whichever metric flatters the conclusion.

The metric follows Haas, Miller & Kimbrough (*Electoral Studies* 79, 2022),
who use the same majority/opportunity thresholds to evaluate a different
change to the apportionment basis, so these numbers are comparable to
published work.

## Caveats that bound every number here

1. **Presidential vote, not House vote.** See above.
2. **`enacted_cd113` is one plan, used for four elections.** Courts redrew
   North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Florida and Virginia during the decade, so
   the 2016 and 2020 enacted rows are approximations. The 2012 row is exact.
3. **Two census vintages.** Election data is on 2020 blocks; the enacted plan
   is on 2010 blocks. Joined by nearest centroid; 0.23% of votes lie more than
   1 km from the nearest 2010 block. Splitline plans need no crosswalk at all,
   being half-planes.
4. **Validated against a known answer.** The enacted 2012 scoring returns
   exactly 209 D / 226 R, the published count of Obama-carried districts.
5. **One parameter set.** All of this is `fixed` house size (435), `span`
   metric, `uniform` population model, no DC, no Puerto Rico. Other
   combinations exist in `usa/plans/` and are not scored here.
