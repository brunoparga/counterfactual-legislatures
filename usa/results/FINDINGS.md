# What the counterfactual maps actually produce

Generated by `usa/tools/score_matrix.py`, `demographics.py`,
`compactness.py`, `near_miss_us.py` and `reweight_us.py`. Raw per-district
numbers are in the JSON files beside this one; `index.json` lists every
scoring with its national summary, and `sens/SENSITIVITY.md` says how much of
this survives a change of parameters.

## What is being measured, and what is not

**The vote is presidential, not congressional.** Block-level House returns
barely exist — 26 states in 2024, 21 in 2022, 3 in 2016 — so there is no
national House map at this resolution and there is not going to be.
Presidential vote is the standard substitute in redistricting work and it has
a genuine advantage here: it is the same contest in every district, so it does
not confound districting with incumbency, candidate quality, or uncontested
seats. It measures **the partisan balance of the ground a seat is drawn on**,
not who would have won the seat.

The distinction is not pedantic. In 2012 Obama carried 209 districts under the
enacted map, but Democrats won only 201 seats. An eight-seat gap between "the
district leans D" and "the D candidate wins" is normal, and every number below
is of the first kind.

**"Bias" here means seat share minus vote share**, in percentage points,
positive for Democrats. It is a descriptive quantity, not the partisan-symmetry
measure of the political-science literature, which requires a swing model.

## 1. Splitline removes about two thirds of the pro-Republican bias

2010-census districts, scored four ways. `enacted_cd113` is the plan actually
in force; `splitline_2010` is ours. Same votes, same states, same blocks —
only the lines differ.

| Contest | D vote % | Enacted D | bias | Splitline D | bias | swing | control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2012 pres | 51.86 | 209 | −3.81 | **231** | +1.24 | +22 | R → **D flips** |
| 2016 pres | 51.01 | 202 | −4.57 | 215 | −1.58 | +13 | R → R |
| 2020 pres | 52.18 | 221 | −1.37 | 229 | +0.47 | +8 | D → D |
| 2016-20 composite | 51.68 | 215 | −2.26 | **221** | −0.88 | +6 | R → **D flips** |

Mean absolute bias falls from **3.00 to 1.04 points — a 65% reduction**. The
direction is consistent: splitline moves seats toward Democrats in all four
scorings, by between 6 and 22 seats.

**Two of the four flip chamber control**, both of them cases where Democrats
carried the national two-party presidential vote but the enacted map returned
a Republican-leaning chamber.

### The interesting failure: 2016 does not flip

Democrats carried the 2016 two-party presidential vote 51.0–49.0 and would
still have held only **215 of 435** districts under splitline — three short of
a majority. Splitline moves 13 seats and that is not enough.

This is the result worth dwelling on, because it separates two explanations
that are usually conflated. The Democratic seat deficit is **not** purely a
gerrymandering artifact. A large part of it is **political geography**:
Democratic voters are concentrated in dense urban areas and win those
districts with enormous margins, wasting votes, while Republican margins are
spread more efficiently across suburban and rural districts. A compactness-
maximising, party-blind rule cannot fix that, because the concentration is
real and compact districts respect it.

So the honest summary is: **redistricting reform recovers roughly two thirds
of the measured bias, and the remaining third is geography, not cartography.**

## 2. The 2000-census map, and a caveat about it

| Plan | Contest | D vote % | D districts | bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| splitline_2000 | 2008 pres | 53.56 | 257 | +5.52 |

No enacted comparator: we hold the block equivalency file for the 113th
Congress only, so there is nothing to difference this against. A **+5.5 bias
in the opposite direction** in a Democratic wave year suggests splitline is
not neutral so much as *responsive* — it converts vote swings into seat swings
more aggressively than the enacted maps do, which is a distinct property from
being unbiased and should not be reported as fairness. Confirming it needs the
BEFs for the 108th–112th Congresses.

## 4. Splitline is race-blind and it is not race-neutral

Voting-age population by race and Hispanic origin, PL 94-171 table P4, 2010
census, all 435 districts. Counts are districts in which the group exceeds the
stated share of VAP.

| Group | National VAP % | \>50% enacted | \>50% splitline | change | 40–50% | 30–40% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hispanic | 14.23 | 30 | 22 | **−8** | 9 → 14 | 19 → 23 |
| non-Hisp. White | 67.04 | 338 | 346 | **+8** | 23 → 33 | 32 → 30 |
| non-Hisp. Black | 11.57 | 22 | **5** | **−17** | 9 → 12 | 11 → 26 |
| non-Hisp. Asian | 4.81 | 1 | 0 | −1 | 1 → 2 | 4 → 4 |

**Black-majority districts fall from 22 to 5, a 77% reduction.** Districts
where Black voters are 30–40% of VAP more than double, 11 to 26. The
population is not disappearing; it is being spread across more districts in
concentrations too small to elect a candidate of choice.

This is the mechanism the Voting Rights Act §2 exists to prevent, and it is
the predictable consequence of a rule that cuts on the shortest available
straight line. Majority-minority districts are frequently non-compact by
necessity — they follow a river, a rail corridor, a chain of neighbourhoods —
and an algorithm blind to race by construction cannot see them.

**These two findings are the same phenomenon seen from two sides.** Majority-
minority districts pack the most reliably Democratic voters in the country
into a small number of seats won with 80% margins. Dissolving them is
*simultaneously* why splitline gains Democrats seats and why it destroys Black
descriptive representation. A reform that improves partisan proportionality by
unpacking the Democratic Party's most loyal constituency is not obviously a
reform, and the trade-off should be stated in exactly those terms rather than
resolved by picking whichever metric flatters the conclusion.

The 2020-census splitline map reproduces the pattern: 6 Black-majority and 25
Hispanic-majority districts out of 435, against a national Black VAP of 11.65%.
There is no enacted comparator for that census -- we hold the block
equivalency file for the 113th Congress only -- so the difference cannot be
measured, but the level matches 2010's splitline figure rather than 2010's
enacted one.

The metric follows Haas, Miller & Kimbrough (*Electoral Studies* 79, 2022),
who use the same majority/opportunity thresholds to evaluate a different
change to the apportionment basis, so these numbers are comparable to
published work.

## 5. Splitline is more compact, and where it is not is informative

Compactness is what splitline claims and it had never been measured here.
The metric is the **population-weighted mean distance from a resident to the
population-weighted centroid of their district**, in kilometres — Brian
Olson's BDistricting objective. It is used because it needs no district
geometry: the enacted 113th-Congress plan reaches us as a list of block
assignments with no polygons, so a perimeter-based score like Polsby-Popper
cannot be computed for the map we most need to compare against. This one is
defined on assignments, so both plans are scored identically, on 2010 census
blocks, with no crosswalk anywhere.

Generated by `usa/tools/compactness.py`; per-state figures in
`usa/results/compactness.json`.

| | population-weighted mean distance to district centroid |
|---|---|
| enacted (113th Congress) | 43.50 km |
| splitline (2010 census) | **39.13 km** |
| change | **−10.1%** |

Splitline is more compact in **37 of the 43 multi-district states**, median
−6.8%. But the national figure understates the pattern, because the effect is
concentrated exactly where one would predict:

| state | seats | enacted | splitline | change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| North Carolina | 13 | 50.03 | 35.74 | **−28.6%** |
| Pennsylvania | 18 | 35.72 | 25.67 | **−28.1%** |
| Texas | 36 | 57.27 | 41.48 | **−27.6%** |
| Arizona | 9 | 58.08 | 44.83 | −22.8% |
| Illinois | 18 | 33.20 | 26.60 | −19.9% |
| Maryland | 8 | 28.17 | 23.50 | −16.6% |
| Virginia | 11 | 47.57 | 39.72 | −16.5% |

Those are the maps of the decade that were litigated, redrawn by courts, or
both. The metric was chosen for being computable on both plans, not for
detecting gerrymanders, and it finds them anyway: in the twelve of thirteen
states with ten or more seats where splitline wins, it wins by 4% to 29%.

### Washington is the exception, and it is not noise

Washington is **29.7% *less* compact** under splitline — the largest
divergence in either direction among the big states, and the only ten-seat
state where the enacted map is tighter. Washington's enacted map was drawn by
a bipartisan commission rather than a legislature, so there was no
gerrymander to undo; and the state's population sits in a single dense strip
along Puget Sound with a mountain range and a sparsely populated east behind
it. A rule that cuts on the shortest straight line will run those cuts
through the strip and pair Seattle neighbourhoods with hundreds of kilometres
of the Cascades, which a commission drawing to communities of interest will
not do.

The general form of that: splitline beats a map drawn to win seats and loses
to a map drawn to respect geography. Compactness is not the same objective as
either.

## 6. Which House votes the lines could have decided

The US analogue of the Brazilian near-miss screen, in
`usa/tools/near_miss_us.py` and `usa/tools/reweight_us.py`, over Voteview roll
calls for the 113th to 117th Congresses. Results in
`usa/results/near_miss.json` and `usa/results/reweighted.json`.

**The structural difference from Brazil is the whole method.** In Brazil the
counterfactual changes how many seats a state has, so delegations are resized.
In the US the apportionment is untouched — splitline redraws districts
*within* a state, and the state sends exactly as many members either way.
What changes is which party wins them. So each delegation is resampled at
constant size: where splitline gives Democrats k more districts than the
enacted map did, k Republicans are drawn out of that state's delegation and k
members are drawn in from that state's observed Democrats, with their votes,
their defections and their absences.

**231 is not a seat count.** The 231-vs-209 figures are districts carried by
the Democratic presidential candidate; the real 2012 House was 201 D. What is
transferable is the *difference*, so the difference is what is applied to the
delegation that actually sat.

### The screen

5,679 House roll calls. A candidate is one that is both **close** — within 25
votes of the bar that actually applied — and **party-divided**, the two
parties' yea shares differing by more than 50 points. A vote where both
parties split evenly does not respond to a change in party composition however
close it was.

The bar is not always a simple majority. Suspension of the rules and veto
overrides need two thirds, and a suspension motion carried 290–140 *fails*;
scoring it as a 150-vote win would have put dozens of comfortable losses in
the candidate set. 37 of the candidates are at the two-thirds bar.

**2,183 roll calls survive both screens**, 537 of them in the 113th Congress.

### What moves

| congress | actual | delta | counterfactual | control | candidates | reverse |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 113th | 201 D / 234 R | +22 | 223 D / 212 R | R → **D** | 537 | **371** |
| 114th | 188 D / 247 R | +22 | 210 D / 225 R | R → R | 195 | 76 |
| 115th | 194 D / 241 R | +13 | 207 D / 228 R | R → R | 571 | 53 |
| 116th | 235 D / 200 R | +13 | 248 D / 187 R | D → D | 414 | 2 |
| 117th | 222 D / 213 R | +8 | 230 D / 205 R | D → D | 466 | 5 |

507 of the 2,183 reverse more likely than not — 316 that passed would have
failed and 191 that failed would have passed.

**Almost three quarters of that is the 113th Congress**, and the reason is
visible in the table rather than in the model: +22 seats is precisely what
turns 201–234 into 223–212. Once chamber control changes, every party-line
vote near the bar goes with it, which is why 371 of 537 candidates reverse
there and only 2 of 414 in the 116th, where Democrats already held the
majority and the extra seats changed nothing that was close.

Among the reversals: the **American Health Care Act**, passed 217–213 in May
2017; the **Agriculture and Nutrition Act of 2018**, passed 213–211; the
**Student Success Act**, passed 218–213; and, in the other direction, the
2014 Veterans' Access to Care Act, which failed 205–207.

### What P(flip) = 1.000 does and does not mean

It means the counterfactual delegation clears the bar in every one of 20,000
draws. The only uncertainty modelled is *sampling* — which members leave and
what the arrivals do — and 22 seats against a two-vote margin swamps that.
It is not behavioural uncertainty. The model assumes a member arriving in a
converted district votes like their new party's other members from the same
state, and that assumption does not come with an error bar. Read these as
"the seat arithmetic no longer supports this outcome", not as a forecast.

Two things are checked rather than assumed. The recomputed yea and nay counts
reproduce Voteview's published totals on **all 5,679 roll calls** — which
required noticing that Voteview records presidential position-taking as cast
votes on 574 of them, and that territorial delegates and independents do count
in the published totals. And with an all-zero delta the resampling returns
every recorded tally exactly, with zero variance, so all the spread reported
comes from seats that actually moved rather than from the machinery.

## Caveats that bound every number here

1. **Presidential vote, not House vote.** See above.
2. **`enacted_cd113` is one plan, used for four elections.** Courts redrew
   North Carolina, Pennsylvania, Florida and Virginia during the decade, so
   the 2016 and 2020 enacted rows are approximations. The 2012 row is exact.
3. **Two census vintages.** Election data is on 2020 blocks; the enacted plan
   is on 2010 blocks. Joined by nearest centroid; 0.23% of votes lie more than
   1 km from the nearest 2010 block. Splitline plans need no crosswalk at all,
   being half-planes.
4. **Validated against a known answer.** The enacted 2012 scoring returns
   exactly 209 D / 226 R, the published count of Obama-carried districts.
5. **One parameter set, now tested.** All of this is `fixed` house size
   (435), `span` metric, `uniform` population model, no DC, no Puerto Rico.
   The model and metric axes have since been scored and the headline holds at
   63-65% across all three available combinations — see
   `sens/SENSITIVITY.md`. The house-size axis is *not* measurable with the
   election data we hold, and the gate catches it rather than letting it
   through: the seven states with no block-level election data are exactly
   the one-district states.
6. **The roll-call model has no behavioural uncertainty.** It resamples which
   members sit, not how a different member would have voted. See section 6.
7. **Compactness is one metric.** Distance-to-centroid is defined on
   assignments, which is why it can score the enacted plan at all, but it is
   not the perimeter-based family most of the literature uses and the two do
   not always agree.
