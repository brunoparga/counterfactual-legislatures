# counterfactual-legislatures

This repository contains code and output data for a political science project. The central idea is: what would be the results of the political process if the electoral system was different?

The expectation is to find in the economics and political science literature studies showing differential impacts of public policies. An example would be "a tobacco advertising ban leads to a 3% decrease in deaths by lung cancer 10 years later". This combines with another question: suppose a country had such a ban in its agenda at one point, but it was eventually not implemented. Which political forces supported it, and which ones opposed it?

And a third question arises: if the election that produced the legislature that failed to ban cigarette ads had been conducted with a different method, what would be its outcome? It is likely that in several situations the government and opposition parties would have been switched, relative to the result that actually happened. Thus we might imagine that, in our hypothetical country, a different electoral system leads to an earlier tobacco ads ban, bringing forward the benefit of reduced cancer deaths.

Evidently, this is highly speculative. But on the other hand, the effects of policies - to be discovered by literature search - can be very significant, such that even a small and confounded probability of a different outcome still leads to considerable expected value. Besides, a parliamentary term deals not with only one policy, but hundreds; it is conceivable that, at least in some occasions, the counterfactual effects of the electoral system on societal well-being build up in similar directions across many policies. The study potentially also replicates across many democratic countries; all in all, the gains from choosing this or that electoral system might compound to an appreciable extent.

## Types of alternative election systems considered

Two of the most important questions defining electoral systems are district magnitude and proportionality. A district in this context means a group of candidates who all appear in the same ballot, and the group of voters that see that ballot on election day and choose from those candidates. In nationwide elections such as for President, the entire country is the district, and for electing a state governor, the entire state; but for legislative elections, where many candidates are elected at the same time, districts range in size from just one winner to the entire country.

Usually, single-winner districts are not concerned with *proportionality*; in a situation where the Purple Party and the Orange League have broad support nationally, but concentrated e.g. in urban versus rural areas, while the Grey Alliance sees its substantial electorate geographically spread more or less evenly, we can see the Purples and the Oranges win a majority of seats in their respective "strongholds", while the Greys are left with very few elected candidates or even none. This effect is compounded in polities that use multi-member districts and consider as winners purely the candidates with the most individual votes.

To address this issue, most democracies implement some form of *proportional representation* (PR). These systems, almost always predicated on multi-winner districts, take into account the party affiliation of each candidate; votes cast for a given party's candidates (or, in some systems, the party itself) are tallied and the seats in the legislature are distributed to each of the parties in proportion to their vote share. In the hypothetical scenario above, if the Grey Alliance gets, say, 15% of the vote, it can be assured to receive approximately 15% of the seats in parliament.

Our investigation will attempt to vary countries' electoral systems along both these axes: on the one hand, we can vary district magnitudes, and think of different ways districts are drawn. On the other hand, particularly for proportional systems, we can pick alternative methods to calculate proportionality. Having a fraction of a representative in congress is unfeasible, so parties need to have integer numbers of elected candidates; different PR systems approach this rounding in different ways, and the consequences of this choice might be surprisingly large.

### Redrawing single-member districts

In most countries that use single-member districts, the delimitation of district boundaries is carried out by actors independent of governments and political parties. They are usually tasked with producing district maps that preserve equality of voting power by making district populations as similar as possible, in addition to goals such as making districts contiguous, respecting boundaries of states and other subnational entities, preserving continuity with previous maps and keeping local communities in the same district.

In the United States, however, drawing district maps is often a political activity conducted by the state legislatures. A pathology called *gerrymandering* often arises: elected representatives or their partisan agents draw the map of the districts to benefit themselves and their party. (Explicitly so, as made clear in the 2026 Supreme Court case *[Louisiana v. Callais](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11431)*). We examine alternative methods that draw district boundaries based on objective algorithms. To the extent that gerrymandering tilts the playing field in favour of the party doing it, we can expect that more neutral processes will produce significantly different maps; in close elections, this might tip the overall control of the legislature to the other party.

### Switching between single-member districts and proportional representation

One of the most consequential changes for the number of parties elected and the consequent process of forming a governing majority is the switch between non-proportional single-member districts and some form of proportional representation. In the former system, a single party often has a majority of the seats even without a majority of the votes, and sometimes not even a plurality. (We say "majority" to mean "more than all others combined" and "plurality" with the meaning of "more than any single competitor".) Under the latter method, there are usually several parties represented in the legislature, and a government needs to obtain and maintain the support of a coalition of parties.

There are documented examples of countries making the switch: France, an adopter of single-member districts with *runoff* elections after the first round winnows out the field to only the most popular parties, chose to hold its 1986 general elections under proportional representation. That natural experiment is rare in that it can illuminate both directions of the change. The move towards PR is illustrated by New Zealand, which since 1996 has used a hybrid method where single-member districts coexist with a set of nationwide-elected representatives, whose numbers are chosen such that the partisan makeup of the legislature reflects the overall party votes.

The virtual inexistence of parties besides the Republicans and the Democrats makes an analysis of the United States harder, as it would require building hypothetical parties out of disputes in primary elections that happen to be clearly delineated in ideological terms. Countries such as the United Kingdom do have third parties; while the Conservative and Labour parties often alternate in obtaining outright majorities, the country's third political force, the Liberal Democrats, entered into a government coalition with the Conservatives after the 2010 general election, and since 2024 have held more than 10% of the seats, a record.

## United States

The first focus of the study is on elections for the United States House of Representatives. For most of American history, the size of the House grew as population did; but starting in the 1920s, it has been capped at 435 seats. Seats are allocated to states in proportion to their population, with a floor of one seat; then each state with more than one seat decides how to distribute its seats within its territory.

A pathology often pointed out is that of *gerrymandering*: elected representatives or their partisan agents draw the map of the single-member districts (SMDs) to benefit themselves and their party. It is a well-known result that SMDs, particularly with the "first past the post" voting method used in most of the U.S.A., generate a two-party system; in America, demographic data such as gender, ethnicity, education and income are strong predictors of how someone is likely to vote (especially since there are only two options available). *Gerrymanderers* use this data, in addition to detailed results of previous votes, to figure out which areas are likely to favour or disfavour them; the latter get concentrated into as few districts as possible, removing them as competitors in the remaining districts, with the result of the gerrymandering party winning a large share of seats.

Proposals to reform this system have been made; we reimplement one such proposal. The [Center for Range Voting](https://www.rangevoting.org/) (CRV) noticed that a necessary condition for gerrymandering is the possibility of drawing arbitrary district boundaries, and one of its effects that ends up quite apparent on a map is that these boundaries end up jagged and elongated (as in the proverbial salamander enacted by Massachusetts governor Elbridge Gerry in 1812. At least the ensuing scandal led the civic-minded voters of the time to torch the man's political career to the ground... but not really. He was actually elected Vice-President of the United States a couple of months later.)

To counter the arbitrariness of district boundaries, the CRV's Warren D. Smith proposes a deterministic, algorithmic system; to counter fluid and elongated shapes that easily include or exclude voters at the representatives' convenience, the algorithm uses only straight lines. Specifically, the recursive process begins by dividing the state with the shortest possible line that leaves an equal population on each side (or, in the case of an odd number of seats, proportional populations, e.g. 2:1 or 5:4). Each half, as long as it still corresponds to more than one district, is similarly cut by the shortest line, until the map is divided in the correct number of districts.

The work currently done in `./splitline` aims to reproduce and extend [the CRV's work](https://www.rangevoting.org/SplitLR.html). First, their Windows code ([autodistrict](https://sourceforge.net/projects/autodistrict/), by Ivan Ryan, 2007) was ported by Claude Code to Linux. 2000 U.S. Census data was pulled from the [Internet Archive](https://web.archive.org/), with the aim to reproduce the CRV's maps - shortest splitline divisions of each of the 50 states, plus the 432 seats of the contiguous 48 states districted in one go, disregarding state lines.

Once that was achieved, map variants were introduced. First, the 2010 and 2020 censuses, with their respective apportionments of seats to each state. There are technical details on how to generate the maps (e.g. should the length of the splitline on inland waters like the Great Lakes count?), variations on House size (the smallest state rule, a.k.a. the Wyoming rule) and inclusion of U.S. residents currently unrepresented by voting members of Congress (allocating seats to the District of Columbia and/or Puerto Rico).
